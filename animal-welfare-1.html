Animal welfare and why it matters

Animal welfare is a measure of an animal's quality of life. This part refines that sentence and explains why it deserves attention.

Three definitions, from simple to formal

Welfare is a spectrum

Welfare is a matter of degree. An animal's state can be very poor, very good, or anywhere between, and its position can change over weeks or within a single day.

Two consequences follow. "Does this animal have welfare?" has no yes or no answer. The answerable question is where the animal sits on the line and what evidence supports that position. Two animals of the same species can sit at different points, and one animal can move along the line as its conditions change.

Welfare of individuals and of populations

Welfare is a state of an individual animal, because only an individual can feel pain, fear or comfort. A population feels nothing. When a text speaks of the welfare of a herd, a flock or a wildlife population, it means the welfare of the individuals in it.

Population measures such as the trend in numbers and the composition describe the health and conservation status of a population. They are useful clues, but indirect: a growing population can still contain many suffering individuals, and a stable one can hide poor welfare.

Why does animal welfare matter?

There are two families of reasons. You should be able to state both and say which one is more persuasive for a given audience.

Reason 1
It matters for the animal itself (intrinsic value). Animals are living beings that can feel pain and suffer, so their condition deserves moral consideration whether or not a person gains anything from it.
Reason 2
It matters for people. Good welfare brings better human-animal interaction, lower cost of disease and injury, better quality and safety of animal-derived food, and better reputation.

Vocabulary: interaction, relationship and bond

Researchers use three different words for how people and animals connect. They are not interchangeable.

Three terms

The distinction goes back to the work of Robert Hinde on social relationships and has been applied to human-animal contact by Hosey and Melfi (2014).

TermAbbreviationMeaningTime scale
Human-animal interactionHAIA single event of contact between a person and an animal.One episode
Human-animal relationshipHARA pattern built from repeated interactions between the same individuals, in which each side comes to expect certain behavior from the other.Weeks to years
Human-animal bondHABA relationship that includes emotional attachment, usually mutual and valued.Long term
Interaction is an event, relationship is a pattern, bond is a relationship with attachment. Every bond is a relationship, and every relationship is built from interactions. The reverse is not true.

Quality matters

A relationship can be positive, neutral or negative from the animal's point of view. Studies of farm animals show that how people handle animals shapes the animal's fear of humans, and fear of humans is linked to stress and to productivity.

4

The Contact-Influence Quadrant

Sort any human-animal situation into one of four zones using two scores: contact and influence.

Each zone points to a different kind of welfare hazard and a different kind of human duty. Many situations have no individual interaction at all: deep-sea animals are an extreme case, and so is wildlife in a landscape that people are clearing. Calling those situations interactions or relationships would be inaccurate, so the quadrant classifies the position of a situation and does not describe an interaction. Human-animal interaction, relationship and bond (HAI, HAR and HAB) from Part 3 describe what happens inside the zones with high contact.

The quadrant covers hazards that originate in human activity, such as housing, handling, pest control and habitat change. Natural events, such as the predation in Image B of the opening, fall outside it.

Hazard
A source of potential harm to welfare, such as heat, rough handling or loss of habitat.
Pathway
The route by which a hazard reaches the animal: through handling and care, or through the environment.
Hazard exposure
How likely an animal is to meet hazards of human origin, given the structure of the situation.
Welfare consequence
The effect on the animal, described by its severity, duration, number of animals affected and reversibility.

The two axes of the quadrant indicate hazard exposure only. They say nothing about consequences.

The four zones

The horizontal axis is contact: how much direct exposure people and the animal have to each other. The vertical axis is influence: how much people shape the animal's resources, space and life events. Select a zone to read its definition, or place the two deer from the opening.

Positions change

A case sits at a position only for a given moment and a given human viewpoint. Choose a scenario to replay the change on the map, then name what changed and which hazard appears in the new zone.

Limits of the model

Knowing the limits protects you from over-trusting a score. Open each one.

Contact counts quantity, and quality is not scored
Much gentle handling and much rough handling receive the same contact score. Research on handling shows that quality matters a great deal.
Zones show exposure, not outcome
A Custody Zone animal can have excellent welfare, and an animal in Remote Zone can suffer. The zone says where to look first.
The model does not capture scale or reversibility
Harm to one individual and harm to a whole population differ morally and practically, and some harms (extinction, habitat loss) cannot be undone. A forest fire set to clear land shows this. The fire sits in the Indirect Zone because people rarely meet the animals, yet the number of animals harmed and the irreversibility of the harm can far exceed any single Custody case. The zone describes the structure of human contact and influence, not the severity of harm.
The score depends on the viewpoint and the case definition
That is why every case starts by naming one group of animals, one routine and one viewpoint. The same logic applies to timing: a typical week hides brief, intense events such as transport, slaughter and ceremonies, so define those cases as the event itself.
This is a teaching tool
Its reliability and validity have not been tested. A suggested test is to have several raters score a set of 12 to 16 case vignettes independently and then measure their agreement. Treat the answer key as the instructor's judgment, not an authority.

Roles of animals in human life

Animals in Custody Zone are kept for a purpose, and the purpose decides what people demand from the animal.

Why people use animals

People gain four kinds of benefit from animals, and each benefit creates one or more roles. Open one benefit at a time.

When roles create welfare hazards

A role places demands on the animal: grow fast, pull a load, stay still, perform on cue, tolerate handling, breed often. The animal also has needs: nutrition, a suitable environment, health care, the chance to behave naturally, and a positive mental state. Two features of roles seem to make welfare problems more likely.

Modifier 1: conflict between demands and needs
The larger the clash, the more likely a welfare problem, even when the animal has only one role. A broiler chicken has a single role (meat), yet the demand for very fast growth at high density clashes strongly with leg health, comfort and movement.
Modifier 2: role stacking
When one animal fills several roles at once, the demands can compete. A family dog that is a companion, a night guard and a breeding animal may be kept indoors one day and chained outside the next, and may be bred repeatedly.

Role audit

Pick an animal, switch on the roles it holds, and match each role to its demand and its clash with needs.

Takeaway. Custody Zone is where hazard exposure starts out highest, and roles help explain which animals within that zone are most exposed.

Optional reading: a short history of animal welfare

The expectation that people should care about animal welfare has grown over time and differs between cultures. No activity or question in this module depends on this reading.

  • Early civilizationsTreated animals mainly as resources for food, clothing and labor. Most societies assumed human superiority, yet some voices urged kinder treatment. One example is ahimsa (non-harm), taught in Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism for more than two thousand years.
  • 17th and 18th centuriesThe Enlightenment brought the idea that animals deserve humane treatment. Animal protection societies, welfare laws and scientific study of animal behavior and welfare followed.
  • 1964Ruth Harrison's book Animal Machines criticized intensive "factory" farming for treating animals like machines.
  • 1965The British government set up an investigation led by Francis William Rogers Brambell. His report ran to 89 pages.
  • 1967 and 1979The Farm Animal Welfare Advisory Committee was formed in 1967. In 1979 it became the Farm Animal Welfare Council, which formulated the "five freedoms" on 5 December 1979.

Violations of welfare: abuse and cruelty

When a person's conduct breaches welfare norms and the animal suffers or is put at risk, we call it abuse or cruelty. This part gives you four ways to classify such conduct.

Words for the same problem

Several terms describe conduct that violates welfare norms, including animal abuse, animal cruelty and animal violence. Usage varies between authors, laws and cultures. In this module, abuse is the general term for any act or omission by a person that breaches welfare norms and causes or risks suffering. Cruelty is used when the conduct is severe or deliberate. The two words overlap in everyday speech, so check how a given law defines each term before relying on it.

Active and passive, physical and mental

Two questions classify any case. Was the harm caused by something a person did (active) or by something a person failed to do (passive)? Did it affect the animal's body (physical) or mind (mental)? Combining the questions gives four types (after Vermeulen and Odendaal, 1993). Read each case and click the cell where it belongs.

Intentional and unintentional, obvious and ambiguous

The Social Media Animal Cruelty Coalition proposed another classification in a 2021 report on cruelty content online. It crosses two questions: was the harm intended, and is it obvious to a viewer that harm is occurring? Select a cell to see its examples.

Where abuse sits on the quadrant

The abuse framework fits some zones better than others. "No abuse" does not mean "no welfare problem": some serious welfare problems arise in Indirect Zone without any person doing anything we would normally call cruel.

Why people fail animals

People fail animals for different reasons, and each reason needs a different remedy. The 4i framework helps you find the main reason before you decide what to do.

Many causes, no single profile

People who are cruel to animals have varied and complex motives, shaped by many factors. Studies link animal cruelty with mental disorders, such as antisocial personality disorder, and with substance misuse, and people who are cruel to animals also tend to commit other crimes and violence against people at higher rates.

Family of causesExamples
PsychologicalA mental disorder such as antisocial personality disorder; desensitization; wanting to dominate an animal's fate for satisfaction.
Knowledge and mindsetNot knowing how to treat an animal well; seeing animals as commodities and believing harm has no real consequence; cultural beliefs that tolerate or encourage cruelty.
SituationalToo little money or resources to meet the animal's needs; peer pressure to show toughness or win acceptance from a group.

This list explains why a single solution cannot work. Teaching a person who already knows what to do will not help, and punishing a person who did not know is unfair and unlikely to work.

The 4i framework

There are four factors by which a person can fail to meet an animal's welfare needs. Telling them apart matters because the remedy differs for each.

FactorMeaningRemedy
IgnoranceThe person does not know what to do, or has no theoretical knowledge of the animal's needs.Education
InexperienceThe person has the knowledge but lacks the skill or practice to do it correctly.Training
IncompetenceThe person has knowledge and experience, yet is unable to do the task.Change of role
InconsiderationThe person has knowledge, experience and ability, and chooses not to act because they do not care.Sanction

Lack of money, time or space (the situational cause above) belongs under ability in most cases.

Find the factor

Pick a case and answer the four questions in order. The chain of requirements breaks at the first question you answer no.

The same act can have different causes

Take a dog left without water. If the owner did not know that dogs need constant access to water, the cause is ignorance. If the owner knew and left anyway, the cause is inconsideration. A teacher or a veterinarian must find out which it is before choosing education or sanction. This is why a good case history asks questions, and why veterinarians are often the right people to ask them.

Optional reading: nine motives for cruelty

Kellert and Felthous (1985), cited in Lockwood and Arkow (2016), described nine motives for cruelty and extreme aggression toward animals. No activity or question in this module depends on this reading.

  1. To control the animal, for example to shape wanted behavior or remove unwanted behavior.
  2. To take revenge on or punish the animal.
  3. To confirm a prejudice against a species or breed.
  4. To express aggression through the animal, for example by making it aggressive.
  5. To raise one's own aggressiveness or to build an aggressive image.
  6. To shock others for amusement.
  7. To take revenge on another person, such as the animal's owner.
  8. To displace hatred from a person seen as superior onto an animal seen as inferior.
  9. Nonspecific sadism, with no particular reason or provocation.

Key messages

  1. Animal welfare is a measure of an animal's quality of life.
  2. Welfare sits on a spectrum, and one animal can sit at different points at different times.
  3. Welfare matters for the animal's own sake and for the sake of people.
  4. Interaction, relationship and bond are different things. The quadrant classifies situations and does not replace those terms.
  5. Contact and influence together locate a situation in one of four zones: Custody, Indirect, Neighbor and Remote.
  6. Positions in the quadrant are not fixed. A change in contact or influence moves a case to another zone.
  7. Zones show where hazard exposure is expected to be highest. They do not show actual welfare.
  8. Roles create demands, and welfare problems become more likely when demands clash with needs.
  9. Science, ethics and law are the three pillars used to judge a situation.
  10. Abuse can be active or passive, physical or mental, intentional or not, obvious or ambiguous.
  11. Ignorance, inexperience, incompetence and inconsideration need different remedies.
